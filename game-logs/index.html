About LegLock

LegLock is a free player-prop research tool for the NBA, NFL and NHL. It takes the raw material of prop research — box scores, game logs, schedules and injury reports — and organises it into one board so you can see, at a glance, which players have been clearing which numbers, how often, and what is likely to affect them next. It exists for fans who enjoy the research side of props: the streaks, the splits and the matchup homework, without a sportsbook attached.

LegLock is a sports statistics and information site. Nothing here is gambling or betting advice — the statistics, averages and hit rates are historical information provided for research and entertainment only, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

What you can do here

  • Props board. The main board has three segments. Top Props ranks player props by hit rate. Streaks groups players by their current over or under streaks. Watchlist flags single-game performances that came in far above a player’s own season average — often the first sign of a bigger role.
  • Live. Scores for every league, updating about every 20 seconds while games are in progress, with the tracked players to watch in each game and how close they are to their lines.
  • Risers. Players whose recent form has jumped well above a cold longer-term baseline — lower-profile names trending up before the market of public opinion catches on.
  • Shuffle Picks. A random deal drawn from the highest hit-rate props, for when you want a fresh set of names to research rather than the same top of the board.
  • Game Logs. A searchable player index. Tap any player to open the full game-log popup: their line, hit rates, a per-game chart and every individual game line.
  • Injuries. The current injury report for the selected league, newest first, so a prop is never researched without checking who is actually available.

Where the numbers come from

All statistics, schedules, scores and injury statuses come from publicly available sports data and are refreshed regularly. Player headshots and team information come from the same public sources. LegLock adds the organisation and the calculations on top of that data — nothing on the board is invented or estimated by hand.

Research lines are not sportsbook odds

This distinction matters, so we state it plainly. Every player line on LegLock is a research line: it is calculated from that player’s own last-10-game average, rounded to the nearest half point. It is not a price set by a sportsbook, it is not available to bet on here, and it will often differ from the line a sportsbook is offering for the same player on the same night. Research lines exist to frame a question — “how often has this player cleared his own recent average?” — not to quote a market. Where game odds (spread, total, moneyline) appear next to a game, they are the listed odds for the game, provided by DraftKings, shown for context only. LegLock has no sportsbook links and takes no bets.

Hit rates are history, not promises

The L5, L10 and L20 percentages on every card describe the past: in that player’s last 5, 10 or 20 games, how often the stat landed on the side of the line shown. A 90% hit rate means it happened nine times out of ten in that window — it does not mean there is a 90% chance it happens tonight. Minutes change, opponents change, injuries happen, and small samples mislead. LegLock’s job is to make the history easy to read; what you conclude from it is up to you. Getting Started

LegLock puts NBA, NFL and NHL player-prop research on one board. There is nothing to install and no account to create — open the page and the board is already built from the latest data. This guide walks through a first visit in the order most people actually use the site.

1. Pick your league

The three pills at the top — NBA, NFL, NHL — switch everything below them: the games strip, the prop board, the player index and the injury report. Each league has its own stat menu. Points, rebounds and assists headline the NBA; passing, rushing and receiving yards lead the NFL; goals, assists, points and shots lead the NHL, with saves for goaltenders.

2. Read the games strip

Under the search bar sits a strip of game boxes for the selected league, ordered with live games first, then finals, then upcoming games. Each box shows the teams, the score if the game has started, and a status line — tip-off or kickoff time for upcoming games, the period and clock for live ones, Final for finished ones. Games in progress get a red outline. Tapping any game box opens the Players to Watch popup for that game, which is the fastest way to see which tracked players are involved and how they are doing.

3. Work the Props board

The Props tab is the home board. Start with the stat chips to narrow to one stat, or leave it on All. The three segments under the heading cut the board three ways: Top Props ranks by hit rate, Streaks groups players by current streaks, and Watchlist surfaces players whose recent single-game marks spiked well above their season averages. The Filters button at the top adds a minimum L10 hit rate and lets you switch overs and unders on or off.

4. Tap a player

Almost everything with a player’s name on it is tappable, and every tap opens the same thing: the game-log popup. It leads with a summary strip — the player’s research line with a stepper to test nearby lines, the over/under direction, and hit rates for the last 5, 10 and 20 games — followed by a per-game chart and a compact list of individual games. Splits and head-to-head numbers against the next opponent sit in collapsed sections at the bottom when you want more depth. Our Prop Board guide walks through every element.

5. Follow along live, then check injuries

While games are on, the Live tab shows every league’s scores in one place, refreshing about every 20 seconds, with the players to watch in each game and live progress toward their lines. Before trusting any prop, finish on the Injuries tab — it mirrors the injury report for the league, newest first — and the Game Logs tab, which ranks the top 25 players per stat category when you want to start from a player rather than a prop.

A simple routine

  • Pick the league and scan the games strip for tonight’s matchups.
  • On the Props board, filter to the stat you care about and sort by the segment that fits — Top Props for consistency, Streaks for momentum, Watchlist for emerging roles.
  • Open two or three game-log popups and check the L10 and L20 hit rates, not just the L5.
  • Cross-check the Injuries tab and the player’s minutes trend before drawing a conclusion.

That is the whole loop. Everything else on the site — Shuffle Picks, Risers, the head-to-head panels — is a variation on it, and the remaining guides explain each piece.

How Hit Rates Work

The hit rate is the single most-used number on LegLock, and the most commonly misunderstood. This guide defines it exactly: what is counted, over which games, against which line, and what the resulting percentage does and does not mean.

The definition

A hit rate answers one narrow question: in a defined set of past games, how often did a player’s stat finish on the chosen side of the line? For an over, a game counts as a hit when the stat is strictly greater than the line; for an under, when it is strictly less. Landing exactly on the line — a push — is not counted as a hit for either side. The rate is simply hits divided by games in the window, shown as a percentage, with the number of games always displayed beside it.

The three windows: L5, L10 and L20

Every prop card shows three windows. L5 covers the player’s last 5 games, L10 the last 10, and L20 the last 20. They are nested — the L5 games are inside the L10, which are inside the L20 — so the three numbers are three readings of the same recent history at different zoom levels. L5 reacts fastest to a hot or cold week and is the noisiest. L20 is the steadiest and slowest to move. L10 sits between them, which is one reason the board’s lines are built from it. When all three windows agree, the pattern has held across a decent stretch. When L5 is far above L20, something changed recently — a bigger role, a weaker run of opponents, a teammate’s injury — and the game-log popup is where you find out what.

Where the line comes from

Hit rates need a line to measure against, and LegLock’s lines are research lines: the player’s own average over their last 10 games, rounded to the nearest half point. A player averaging 24.3 points over his last 10 gets a research line of 24.5. Measuring a player against his own recent average makes the hit rate a statement about consistency — how regularly he plays above or below his current level — rather than a statement about any sportsbook’s opinion. The stepper in the game-log popup lets you move the line up or down by half points and watch every hit rate recalculate, which is the quickest way to feel how sensitive a rate is to the number it is measured against. The Research Lines vs Real Odds guide covers the difference in full.

What a hit rate is not

  • It is not a probability. An 80% L10 hit rate means the stat cleared the line in 8 of the last 10 games. It does not mean there is an 80% chance it clears tonight — tonight’s opponent, minutes and game context are not in the percentage.
  • It is not sample-proof. 100% over 5 games is five games. The board always shows the game count next to the percentage; weight the two together.
  • It does not see the future on the schedule. Rates are built from games already played, against whoever those opponents happened to be. The head-to-head section in the game-log popup exists precisely to check the rate against the specific team coming up.
  • It ignores availability. A player’s rate says nothing about whether he plays tonight. That is what the Injuries tab is for.

Reading the three numbers together

A useful habit: start at L20 for the baseline, check L10 for the current level the line is built from, then use L5 as a form flag. Rising windows (L5 above L10 above L20) suggest a player trending up; the reverse suggests cooling off. Streaks, on the board’s Streaks segment, are the same idea in a different shape — consecutive games on one side of the line, counted in a row rather than as a percentage. Neither shape is a prediction. Both are organised history, and organised history is all LegLock claims to offer.

Research Lines vs Real Odds

Two different numbers can both be called “the line” for the same player on the same night, and confusing them is the easiest mistake in prop research. LegLock shows one kind. Sportsbooks show the other. This guide explains how each is built, why they differ, and how to use the difference honestly.

What a research line is

A research line is calculated, not priced. LegLock takes a player’s game log, averages the stat over his last 10 games, and rounds to the nearest half point. A guard averaging 7.1 assists over his last 10 gets a research line of 7.5 assists. That is the whole recipe — no trader, no market, no opinion about tonight. Because the line is the player’s own recent average, roughly half of his games land above it and half below, and the hit rate against it measures consistency: does he play at a steady level, or swing between big nights and quiet ones? Research lines are stable, transparent and reproducible — anyone with the same game log gets the same number.

What a real sportsbook line is

A sportsbook’s line is a price set by traders to balance betting action and manage risk. It starts from projections, then moves — sometimes several times in a day — with injury news, lineup announcements, rest, matchups and the weight of money on each side. It also carries the book’s margin, and it is quoted with odds (for example −110) that say what a winning bet pays. Two books can post different numbers for the same player at the same moment, and both can differ from his recent average for good reasons: a star teammate ruled out can push a usage line up before a single game is played.

Why the two numbers rarely match

  • Different inputs. The research line looks backward at 10 games. The book’s line looks forward at tonight’s specific situation.
  • Different purposes. The research line frames a question about consistency. The book’s line exists to be bet on, at a price that includes margin.
  • Different clocks. The research line changes only when a new game enters the last-10 window. The book’s line can move in minutes.

How to use both without fooling yourself

The honest use of a research line is as a baseline. If a player clears his own last-10 average in 80% of his last 20 games, he is a metronome — that is a fact about his history, whatever any book posts. If a sportsbook’s number sits well below that average, the gap is a reason to ask why — maybe the book knows about a minutes limit, a tougher matchup, or a likely blowout — not a reason to assume the book is wrong. Gaps are questions, not conclusions. LegLock itself never blurs the two: player lines here are always research lines, they cannot be bet on this site, and there are no sportsbook links anywhere on the page. The only real market prices shown are the game odds (spread, total, moneyline) listed next to some games, provided by DraftKings, and they are labelled as context for the game — never mixed into a player’s research numbers.

The one-sentence version

A research line tells you what a player has been; a sportsbook line tells you what the market expects tonight, at a price. LegLock deals in the first, on purpose, and says so on every page.

How to Read a Prop Board

A prop card packs a lot into a small space. This guide goes through the board piece by piece — the card itself, the three segments that organise it, the two views that cut it differently (Streaks and Risers), and the game-log popup behind every player’s name — so each number lands with the context it needs.

Anatomy of a prop card

  • Player and team. Name, team and position at the top, with the headshot where one is available.
  • Streak badge. The small box at the top right counts consecutive games the player has finished on the same side of the line — overs or unders in a row. It turns amber when the streak is long enough to be notable.
  • The line box. The shaded band is the prop itself: Over or Under, the research line and the stat (for example, Over 24.5 Points). On the right, the player’s last-10 average for that stat, so the line and the average it came from sit side by side.
  • Hit-rate bars. Three bars — L5, L10, L20 — each showing the percentage of games in that window that landed on the card’s side of the line, with the game count in the label. Green bars are the strongest rates, amber the middle band. The Hit Rates guide explains how to weigh them.
  • The footer note. A short context line, such as the next opponent or a minutes trend, and the Game log button that opens the full popup.

The three segments

Top Props is the default ranking: the board’s props ordered by hit rate, filtered by the stat chips and the Filters panel (stat type, minimum L10 rate, overs and unders). Streaks reorganises the same players into sheets grouped by streak — who is on an over streak, who is on an under streak, and how long each run is — because a run of consecutive results reads differently from a percentage. Watchlist looks for change instead of consistency: single-game marks at least 35% above a player’s own season average within his last four games, the kind of spike that often means a new role, more minutes, or a breakout worth investigating in the game log.

The Risers view

The Risers tab applies almost the opposite filter. It starts with players in the bottom half by last-20 baseline — names the board normally overlooks. In the NBA and NFL there is no pass/fail bar: each qualifying player-stat gets a Riser Score that estimates whether the surge is likely to continue. Consistency carries the most weight (how many of the last 5 and last 10 games beat the baseline), then the current streak, then the size of the jump with the percentage capped so one wild game cannot dominate; in the NBA a real minutes increase — the most persistent signal of all — adds an opportunity boost, including when a higher-minutes teammate is on the injury report; the NFL counts a real usage increase instead (snap share and target or carry share), with the same teammate boost, and each NFL card shows the upcoming opponent’s defensive rank for the stat. The score is scaled down when the evidence behind it is thin, only players with a game today or tomorrow are listed (for the NFL, a game in the current Thursday-to-Monday week), and the top 10 are shown with every component number on the card: baseline against last-5 average, the percentage jump, the last five values with the ones above baseline marked, and the next opponent with the player’s average against that team. The NHL keeps a harder bar instead — last-5 average at least 50% above baseline, with a minimum absolute jump that scales by stat so tiny numbers cannot qualify on a technicality. A riser card is a research lead, not a verdict — the popup behind it shows whether the jump is minutes-driven or a couple of hot shooting nights.

The game-log popup

Every player tap opens the same popup, and it is deliberately summary-first. At the top: the player, his next game, and a stat switcher. Below that, one summary strip holds the research line with a −/+ stepper (move the line and every number recalculates), the Over/Under switch, the L5/L10/L20 hit-rate chips, and a grid of the numbers that matter — window average, record against the line, streak, and the player’s average against his next opponent. A chart plots each game against the line, and under it the game log itself sits in a collapsible section giving one compact line per game: date, opponent, minutes, the stat value, and whether it landed over or under. The deeper material — extra split windows, home/away and head-to-head detail, and the full every-stat table — lives in two more collapsible sections below it. The three sections work as an accordion: opening any one closes the others, so only the section you are reading takes up space.

Putting a read together

A disciplined read of one card takes under a minute: check the L20 bar for the baseline, the L10 for the level the line was built from, the L5 and streak badge for current form, then open the popup to see the minutes trend and the opponent split, and finish with the Injuries tab. If the windows disagree, the popup usually shows why. That sequence — baseline, level, form, context, availability — is the board working as designed.

Responsible Play

LegLock is a statistics and information site. It offers no betting, takes no wagers, holds no accounts and links to no sportsbooks — and nothing on it is gambling or betting advice. The numbers here are historical information for research and entertainment. This guide exists because many readers also bet elsewhere, and the habits that keep research enjoyable are worth stating plainly.

Keep the roles straight

A hit rate describes games already played. It is not a prediction, a tip or a recommendation, and no percentage on this site changes the fact that any single game can go the other way. If you bet, treat research as one input among many — and never as a guarantee, because past performance does not guarantee future results.

Habits that protect you

  • Set limits first. Decide a budget and a time limit before you start, in money you can afford to lose entirely, and stop when you reach either limit.
  • Never chase losses. Betting more to win back what is gone is the fastest route from entertainment to harm. A loss is a cost already paid, not a debt the next bet can collect.
  • Keep it occasional and social. If it stops being fun, that is information — act on it early rather than late.
  • Do not bet when impaired or upset. Alcohol, exhaustion and emotional stress all degrade exactly the judgement betting demands.
  • Know the law where you are. Legal gambling age is 19+ in Ontario and varies by jurisdiction. Follow your local rules.

Warning signs

Spending more time or money than intended, hiding betting from people close to you, borrowing to bet, missing work or bills, or feeling unable to stop are all signs that gambling has become a problem rather than a pastime. They are common, they are nothing to be ashamed of, and help works best early.

Help in Ontario: the ConnexOntario Problem Gambling Helpline — 1-866-531-2600 — is free, confidential and available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.
